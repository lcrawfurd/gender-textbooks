Methods

What is counted

Each book’s text is matched against the paper’s list of gendered nouns and pronouns (“she”, “mother”, “queen”; “he”, “father”, “king”…). The female share is female words divided by female and male words. 130 national textbooks have no gendered words; they count in the headline but add nothing to it. The numbers come from the paper’s method, reimplemented in the tgs software, on a larger set of books, so they differ from the published paper. 1,105 extra copies of books found more than once were left out, so each book is counted once; the list, with how each pair was judged, is in the repository (search/v1/site/duplicates.csv). A few of the paper’s books were filed under the wrong country or grade (a Sri Lankan health book under Pakistan, South African maths books under Solomon Islands, a Grade 6 maths book under Grade 7); each is counted once, where it belongs, and the table notes it. The check against the published paper uses the paper’s books as it filed them. 1,250 files that hold a chapter, section or unit of a book are counted as the 123 books they belong to.

Which books count

The headline and every cross-country result use national textbooks only, the paper’s own rule: official English student textbooks. That is the paper’s corpus plus the student books the 2026 systematic search found on ministry and agency sites and their official mirrors (1,347 books). The 417 Indian state-board and NCERT textbooks among them (2020–23 editions) come from Lee Crawfurd’s 2024 Ideas for India analysis, which took them from an unofficial mirror of the boards’ books (ncertbooks.guru). Teacher guides, readers, programme books and NGO collections are shown beside them, never mixed in. 90 files from Lebanon’s curriculum centre are excerpts (5 to 22 pages each), not whole textbooks; they are shown beside the national textbooks as their own group and are in no national result, so Lebanon is not ranked. For 22 of the search’s books the title names a different kind from the search’s guess from the file (a “Student Textbook” filed as a teacher’s guide, a “Teacher’s Guide” filed as a student book), and the title is taken; for 2 files whose titles contradict their contents, the file is followed.

Subjects and grades

New books’ subject and grade labels are mapped to the paper’s 13 subject groups and to grades 1–13 by rule, then by hand. Of 2,974 national textbooks, 65 have no subject group and 203 have no grade; books missing either count in the headline but not in the adjusted results.

The adjustment

Country and subject shares come from the paper’s regression of each book’s female share on country, subject group, grade and the log of its length (2,633 books with a country, a subject group, a grade and at least one gendered word), summarised as the average prediction for each country or subject (Stata’s margins), with 95% intervals. Countries with fewer than 5 books in the model share one level of the country factor, as the paper’s “Other” did; countries with fewer than 10 are listed, not ranked. The pooled share beside each adjusted share is female words over all gendered words in all of a country’s national textbooks (or of a subject group’s), with no adjustment.

Run on the paper’s own books, the adjusted country shares here differ from the corrected figures (the paper’s own Stata code rerun with an exact word count) by 1.6 points on average across 27 countries (largest: 7.2 points, Kenya), and the subject shares by 1.5 points on average across 13 subject groups (largest: 4.2 points, Arts). The differences reflect the rebuilt corpus and the reimplemented counts. The comparison uses all of the paper’s books, as published (including any the site counts once because they were found more than once). Not compared, because the model has no margin for them here: 1 country (Malawi).

Children and adults

The children and adults figures follow the paper’s Figure 3: each book with at least one gendered word about children and one about adults counts once, with its own female share of each; the chart shows the mean of those shares over books, and the difference (children minus adults) is the mean of each book’s own difference, with a 95% interval from a paired t test that, as in the paper, treats books as independent.

Over time

The time chart compares books within countries: a regression of each book’s share on its country, its period of publication and the log of its length (no subject or grade), on books with a publication year from 2000 on (1,482); 9 books with an earlier year are left out, since some of those years are misread; 97 books with no gendered words are left out (the other 33 are among those with no year). 1,386 national textbooks have no publication year and are not in the time chart. The change per decade comes from a second regression that uses the year, in decades, in place of the period.

Limits

The adjustment holds subject, grade and book length fixed, but not which books each ministry has put online, so a country’s share describes the books found for it, and countries with few books have wide intervals. Only English books are scored: in countries that teach in another language, many of them are English-language courses, so their shares describe those courses rather than the whole curriculum.

Country characteristics

From the World Bank API, the most recent value for each country, if from 2010 or later:

  • GDP per head, PPP (constant international dollars) (NY.GDP.PCAP.PP.KD)
  • Gender parity index of gross secondary school enrolment (SE.ENR.SECO.FM.ZS)
  • Seats held by women in national parliaments (%) (SG.GEN.PARL.ZS)
  • Female labour-force participation (% of women 15+, ILO modelled estimate) (SL.TLF.CACT.FE.ZS)

Each country-characteristics panel is an unweighted least-squares line through the ranked countries’ adjusted shares; it ignores the uncertainty of each share, uses Student’s t for its interval, and is not the paper’s book-level regression (which found a positive link with income).

Embedding the charts

Each chart also has a page of its own, for an iframe on another site: this site’s address followed by one of these paths, with the height to give the frame if nothing else sets it:

  • embed/map.html, height 936
  • embed/countries.html, height 2290
  • embed/subjects.html, height 1129
  • embed/ages.html, height 921
  • embed/time.html, height 755
  • embed/links.html, height 3464
  • embed/collections.html, height 1018

For example, with this site’s address in place of the dots:

<iframe src="…/embed/countries.html" title="Female share of gendered words by country"
  width="100%" height="2290" style="border:0"></iframe>

Each page also tells the page that holds it how tall it is. To have the frames fit their charts, add this once to that page, after the frames:

<script>
addEventListener("message", e => e.data && e.data.tgsEmbed && e.data.height > 0 &&
  document.querySelectorAll("iframe").forEach(f => f.contentWindow === e.source &&
    (f.style.height = e.data.height + "px")));
</script>
