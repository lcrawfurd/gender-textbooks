Why it matters

Textbooks are the one book almost every child reads, often the only one, and teachers and pupils treat them as authoritative. Whether the way they show women and men matters is partly an empirical question. Here is what the research shows, and where it is still thin.

Changing what children read

A few studies change the stories or pictures children see and measure what happens.

  • In 100 government primary schools in Botswana (5,616 pupils in grades 3 to 5), schools were randomly given daily reading lessons with stories of girls in non-stereotyped jobs. In urban schools, fewer pupils agreed that girls cannot do the job they want (about 12 points fewer), and girls’ career hopes moved towards doctor and away from nurse; in rural schools the belief did not change. Reading comprehension rose slightly in later rounds.

    Angrist, Berkhout, Büchel, Dam and Mukoyi (2026), “Stories that Shape Us: Evidence from a Gender-Empowering Literacy Program”, Youth Impact; conference slides, not yet a paper Randomised trial; preliminary

  • US high-school students read the same chemistry lesson illustrated with female or with male scientists. Girls understood the lesson better with the female scientists.

    Good, Woodzicka and Wingfield (2010), “The Effects of Gender Stereotypic and Counter-Stereotypic Textbook Images on Science Performance”, Journal of Social Psychology 150(2) Experiment; short run

  • In Israel, first-graders who learned to read from a gender-fair primer said more activities suit both girls and boys than children using a stereotyped one.

    Karniol and Gal-Disegni (2009), “The Impact of Gender-Fair versus Gender-Stereotyped Basal Readers on 1st-Grade Children’s Gender Stereotypes”, Journal of Research in Childhood Education 23(4) Natural experiment; two schools

  • US preschool boys persisted longer at a task after hearing a story in which a boy achieved something.

    McArthur and Eisen (1976), “Achievements of male and female storybook characters as determinants of achievement behavior by boys and girls”, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 33(4) Experiment; small

What schools teach changes beliefs and results

The content of the curriculum, and the views of the teachers who deliver it, shape what children believe and how they do.

  • China’s textbook reform reached provinces in different years. Students who studied the new textbooks held different political views, including more trust in officials. It is about politics, not gender, but it is the clearest evidence that textbook content changes beliefs.

    Cantoni, Chen, Yang, Yuchtman and Zhang (2017), “Curriculum and Ideology”, Journal of Political Economy 125(2) Causal (staggered reform)

  • In Turkey, primary-school girls taught for over a year by teachers with traditional gender views scored lower in maths and reading; boys were unaffected.

    Alan, Ertac and Mumcu (2018), “Gender Stereotypes in the Classroom and Effects on Achievement”, Review of Economics and Statistics 100(5) Causal (random assignment of teachers)

  • In Italy, girls whose maths teachers held stronger gender–science stereotypes fell further behind boys in maths and chose less demanding high schools.

    Carlana (2019), “Implicit Stereotypes: Evidence from Teachers’ Gender Bias”, Quarterly Journal of Economics 134(3) Causal

  • In Israel, primary teachers who marked boys more generously raised boys’ later results, lowered girls’, and made girls less likely to take advanced maths and science.

    Lavy and Sand (2018), “On the origins of gender gaps in human capital”, Journal of Public Economics 167 Causal

Who children see changes what they aim for

Seeing women in roles they would not otherwise see, in person or on screen, raises girls’ aspirations and schooling.

  • In West Bengal, where village council seats were randomly reserved for women, two terms of a woman leader closed the gender gap in teenagers’ aspirations by a third and erased the gap in their schooling.

    Beaman, Duflo, Pande and Topalova (2012), “Female Leadership Raises Aspirations and Educational Attainment for Girls”, Science 335(6068) Randomised policy

  • Ugandan secondary students shown a film about a girl who becomes a chess champion did better in national exams, especially maths, and girls were more likely to stay in school.

    Riley (2024), “Role Models in Movies: The Impact of Queen of Katwe on Students’ Educational Attainment”, Review of Economics and Statistics 106(2) Randomised trial

  • Classroom visits by women college students made Somali primary pupils’ gender attitudes more equal; years later, more went on to high school.

    Kipchumba, Porter, Serra and Sulaiman (2024), “The Impact of Role Models on Youths’ Aspirations, Gender Attitudes and Education in Somalia”, IZA Discussion Paper 17261 Randomised trial; working paper

  • Two short classroom visits by successful women economists raised the share of US women students majoring in economics by 8 points, from 9%.

    Porter and Serra (2020), “Gender Differences in the Choice of Major: The Importance of Female Role Models”, American Economic Journal: Applied Economics 12(3) Randomised trial

  • One-hour visits by women scientists to French high schools weakened stereotypes about science jobs and led more high-achieving girls into selective science programmes.

    Breda, Grenet, Monnet and Van Effenterre (2023), “How Effective are Female Role Models in Steering Girls Towards STEM?”, Economic Journal 133(653) Randomised trial

  • As cable television reached Indian villages, acceptance of domestic violence and son preference fell and women’s autonomy rose.

    Jensen and Oster (2009), “The Power of TV: Cable Television and Women’s Status in India”, Quarterly Journal of Economics 124(3) Causal-leaning (staggered rollout)

  • Women in areas reached by Brazil’s soap operas, which showed small families, had fewer children.

    La Ferrara, Chong and Duryea (2012), “Soap Operas and Fertility: Evidence from Brazil”, American Economic Journal: Applied Economics 4(4) Causal-leaning (staggered rollout)

Gender beliefs form early, last, and can be changed at school

  • By age six, US girls are less likely than boys to think people of their own gender are “really, really smart”, and start avoiding games said to be for very smart children.

    Bian, Leslie and Cimpian (2017), “Gender stereotypes about intellectual ability emerge early and influence children’s interests”, Science 355(6323) Descriptive and small experiments

  • In 314 government schools in Haryana, classroom discussions on gender equality raised pupils’ support for it by 0.25 standard deviations, and boys did more chores at home; effects lasted two years on.

    Dhar, Jain and Jayachandran (2022), “Reshaping Adolescents’ Gender Attitudes: Evidence from a School-Based Experiment in India”, American Economic Review 112(3) Randomised trial

  • In the US, the wives of men whose mothers worked were more likely to work themselves: gender roles learned in childhood carry into adult life.

    Fernández, Fogli and Olivetti (2004), “Mothers and Sons: Preference Formation and Female Labor Force Dynamics”, Quarterly Journal of Economics 119(4) Causal-leaning

What textbooks show

  • Women and girls are mentioned less than men and boys, and are shown as more passive and more tied to the home; the gap is widest in South Asia. This site extends that paper.

    Crawfurd, Saintis-Miller and Todd (2024), “Sexist textbooks: Automated analysis of gender bias in 1,255 books from 34 countries”, PLOS ONE 19(10) Descriptive

  • In government English textbooks, 24% of characters in Pakistan’s books are female, against 40% across the four countries; women appear mostly in domestic, low-prestige roles.

    Islam and Asadullah (2018), “Gender stereotypes and education: A comparative content analysis of Malaysian, Indonesian, Pakistani and Bangladeshi school textbooks”, PLOS ONE 13(1) Descriptive

  • A review for UNESCO: gender bias in textbooks is near-universal, strikingly similar across countries, and slow to change.

    Blumberg (2008), “The invisible obstacle to educational equality: gender bias in textbooks”, Prospects 38(3) Review

  • Many countries’ secondary textbooks still fail to cover gender equality fully or fairly, and teachers and pupils treat textbooks as authoritative.

    UNESCO Global Education Monitoring Report (2016), “Textbooks pave the way to sustainable development”, Policy Paper 28 Descriptive

The bottom line

Well established: textbooks across the world, and above all in South Asia, show fewer women than men and put them in narrower roles; children’s ideas about gender form early; and what schools teach, and the women children see, change their beliefs, aspirations and schooling.

Not yet established: whether the gender content of textbooks on its own changes children’s long-run outcomes. Direct tests are few, small or short-run, and the one large trial in an African school system (Botswana) is not yet published.

So: biased textbooks are common and cheap to fix, and the wider evidence makes harm plausible. Reforms should be evaluated as they are rolled out.
