Methods

What is counted

Each book’s text is matched against the paper’s list of gendered nouns and pronouns (“she”, “mother”, “queen”; “he”, “father”, “king”…). The female share is female words divided by female and male words. 132 national textbooks have no gendered words; they count in the headline but add nothing to it. The numbers come from the paper’s method, reimplemented in the tgs software, on a larger set of books, so they differ from the published paper. 1,355 extra copies of books found more than once were left out, so each book is counted once; the list, with how each pair was judged, is in the repository (search/v1/site/duplicates.csv). A few of the paper’s books were filed under the wrong country or grade (a Sri Lankan health book under Pakistan, South African maths books under Solomon Islands, a Grade 6 maths book under Grade 7); each is counted once, where it belongs, and the table notes it. The check against the published paper uses the paper’s books as it filed them. 1,504 files that hold a chapter, section or unit of a book are counted as the 141 books they belong to.

Which books count

The headline and every cross-country result use national textbooks only, the paper’s own rule: official English student textbooks. That is the paper’s corpus plus the student books the 2026 systematic search found on ministry and agency sites and their official mirrors (1,617 books). The 399 Indian state-board and NCERT textbooks among them (2020–23 editions) come from Lee Crawfurd’s 2024 Ideas for India analysis, which took them from an unofficial mirror of the boards’ books (ncertbooks.guru). Teacher guides, readers, programme books and NGO collections are shown beside them, never mixed in. 90 files from Lebanon’s curriculum centre are excerpts (5 to 22 pages each), not whole textbooks; they are shown beside the national textbooks as their own group and are in no national result, so Lebanon is not ranked. For 22 of the search’s books the title names a different kind from the search’s guess from the file (a “Student Textbook” filed as a teacher’s guide, a “Teacher’s Guide” filed as a student book), and the title is taken; for 2 files whose titles contradict their contents, the file is followed.

Subjects and grades

New books’ subject and grade labels are mapped to the paper’s 13 subject groups and to grades 1–13 by rule, then by hand. Of 3,226 national textbooks, 118 have no subject group and 270 have no grade; books missing either count in the headline but not in the adjusted results.

The adjustment

Country and subject shares come from the paper’s regression of each book’s female share on country, subject group, grade and the log of its length (2,806 books with a country, a subject group, a grade and at least one gendered word), summarised as the average prediction for each country or subject (Stata’s margins), with 95% intervals. Countries with fewer than 5 books in the model share one level of the country factor, as the paper’s “Other” did; countries with fewer than 10 are listed, not ranked. The pooled share beside each adjusted share is female words over all gendered words in all of a country’s national textbooks (or of a subject group’s), with no adjustment. Within India and the United States, the same regression is run on that country’s books alone, with state in place of country (books with no state are a group of their own), for countries whose books come from at least two states with 10 or more books in the model each; states with fewer are listed, not estimated.

Run on the paper’s own books, the adjusted country shares here differ from the corrected figures (the paper’s own Stata code rerun with an exact word count) by 1.6 points on average across 27 countries (largest: 7.2 points, Kenya), and the subject shares by 1.5 points on average across 13 subject groups (largest: 4.2 points, Arts). The differences reflect the rebuilt corpus and the reimplemented counts. The comparison uses all of the paper’s books, as published (including any the site counts once because they were found more than once). Not compared, because the model has no margin for them here: 1 country (Malawi).

Children and adults

The children and adults figures follow the paper’s Figure 3: each book with at least one gendered word about children and one about adults counts once, with its own female share of each; the chart shows the mean of those shares over books, and the difference (children minus adults) is the mean of each book’s own difference, with a 95% interval from a paired t test that, as in the paper, treats books as independent.

Over time

The time chart compares books within countries: a regression of each book’s share on its country, its period of publication and the log of its length (no subject or grade), on books with a publication year from 2000 on (1,677); 9 books with an earlier year are left out, since some of those years are misread; 99 books with no gendered words are left out (the other 33 are among those with no year). 1,441 national textbooks have no publication year and are not in the time chart. The change per decade comes from a second regression that uses the year, in decades, in place of the period.

Earlier editions

Earlier editions are older editions of books the site holds in a newer one: pre-2022 NCERT books, Rwanda’s 2014–15 readers, and Punjab’s books from before the Single National Curriculum, found as Internet Archive and Wayback copies of the publishers’ own files. A book’s year is the year printed in it where it shows one, otherwise the year it was captured. They count in the time chart and the same-title table only, not in the headline or any other result, which describe current books. Where the site holds two editions of one title, the older is an earlier edition (the duplicate table marks the pair “edition”): so far Maharashtra’s 2006–13 textbooks, whose newer editions the site counts instead. NCERT’s Class 1–8 books were replaced under the 2023 curriculum framework by new titles rather than revised, so they have no pair in the table and count in the time chart only. 170 are in the time chart.

Limits

The adjustment holds subject, grade and book length fixed, but not which books each ministry has put online, so a country’s share describes the books found for it, and countries with few books have wide intervals. Only English books are scored: in countries that teach in another language, many of them are English-language courses, so their shares describe those courses rather than the whole curriculum.

Country characteristics

From the World Bank API, the most recent value for each country, if from 2010 or later:

  • GDP per head, PPP (constant international dollars) (NY.GDP.PCAP.PP.KD)
  • Gender parity index of gross secondary school enrolment (SE.ENR.SECO.FM.ZS)
  • Seats held by women in national parliaments (%) (SG.GEN.PARL.ZS)
  • Female labour-force participation (% of women 15+, ILO modelled estimate) (SL.TLF.CACT.FE.ZS)

Each country-characteristics panel is an unweighted least-squares line through the ranked countries’ adjusted shares; it ignores the uncertainty of each share, uses Student’s t for its interval, and is not the paper’s book-level regression (which found a positive link with income).

Stereotype measures

The three stereotype sections measure what the 2024 paper measured for its whole corpus, now book by book for the 3,226 national textbooks (results of 9 November 2026), so that they can be pooled by region and income group. Only counts leave each book; no text is kept or published.

Words near women and men. For each mention of a woman or a man (the site’s word lists), the words of the paper’s four themes (achievement, appearance, family and the home, work; from Lucy et al. 2020, Adukia et al. 2023 and Kumar et al. 2021, 50 words in all) in the same sentence and up to ten words either side, per 100 mentions.

How they are described. The adjectives that modify a gendered noun (a kind woman) or follow a gendered subject and a linking verb (she is kind), and the verbs whose subject is a gendered person (she cooked), found by the spaCy parser and scored with the NRC Valence, Arousal and Dominance lexicon (Mohammad 2018), each from 0 to 1, averaged over every use. Dominance stands in for agency, as in the paper.

The jobs they are shown in. The paper’s 1,155 occupation titles, counted in sentences that mention only women or only men (the paper’s rule). The 152 commonest titles, which cover 96% of the mentions, were mapped by hand to the nine ISCO-08 major groups; titles that are not jobs in context (student, retired), too vague (assistant, agent), or mostly something else (actor, as in economic actors; writer, as in the author of a passage) are left out. Women’s share of each group’s employment comes from ILOSTAT (employment by sex and occupation, the latest year with all nine groups), for the book’s country, or the world where ILOSTAT has no figure. The mapping is in the repository (research/stereo/lists/occupation_isco.csv).

Estimates. Each value pools the counts of the books it covers. Intervals come from 500 resamples of whole countries (books in one country are alike); a country’s own interval resamples its books. Each measure states the least difference between two countries that the data can detect, and only countries whose interval is narrower than it are ranked. The research files (research/stereo/estimates.csv) also give each country with at least 10 national textbooks a value shrunk towards the mean of all countries in proportion to its noise (an empirical Bayes step); the site shows each country’s own value.

Three methods, one answer. Counting theme words near a mention is the main measure. Two checks use word vectors instead: the similarity of the words around each mention to each theme in GloVe vectors trained on other text (300 dimensions), and the paper’s own method, word2vec trained on each country’s textbooks where a country has 1.5 million words or more (11 countries, 50 runs on resampled sentences each). Positive numbers mean the theme sits nearer women.

Pictures

A model (Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite) looks at 30 pages drawn at random from each national textbook, in whatever language it is written, and records every person pictured on them: gender (where it can tell), age group, role, whether a main or background figure, activity, setting, dress, head covering, any visible disability or aid, and whether the person is a real, named figure. It also records whether each page’s pictures are drawings or photos and whether the scene looks local. It follows a written coding manual, version 3 (docs/reference/image-coding-manual-v3.md in the repository), which defines each code. For most countries the coding runs in two steps: a first, cheap look at each sampled page asks only whether it shows any person, and only the pages where it does are coded in full (in a test on Pakistan’s coded pages, the first look missed pages holding 1.6% of the people pictured). Coded so far: Pakistan (367 national textbooks, 10,984 pages); Rwanda (95 national textbooks, 760 pages). Other countries follow; until then, the picture figures describe the national textbooks of the 2 countries coded so far only.

Second copies of a book (the same book found twice) are counted once. Every disability flag is checked by eye, and only confirmed flags count: of 64 people flagged, 6 are false alarms. A share is shown only with at least 30 people whose gender the model could tell; thinner cells keep their count and show a dash. The model’s answers are not published, only the figures made from them.

Skin tone is measured three ways, each in three bands (lighter, medium, darker); the shares are of the people whose tone could be judged, and black-and-white pictures get no tone. Photos and drawings are reported apart, since a drawing’s colours are the illustrator’s choice. The main measure is judged by eye from the face alone: each face large and clear enough to cut out of the picture is shown to the model on its own, with nothing else of the page, and judged against a short manual (docs/reference/skin-tone-face-manual.md). This covers about a third of the people pictured. The second measure is judged by eye with the whole page visible, in a second pass over the pages with people. It covers more people, but the page sways it: on the same faces, the role, dress and setting around a person change how light or dark they are judged, so it is reported only as a check, by how often it agrees with the face-only judgement. The third measure comes from the pixels of each face: the individual typology angle of the skin pixels, following Adukia et al. (2023). The site reports only what it finds for photos against drawings, not its shares: shadows, and faded or sepia photos, darken the pixels of a face, so it puts too many people in the darker band, above all in photos. Skin tone is shown where it has been measured.

Embedding the charts

Each chart also has a page of its own, for an iframe on another site: this site’s address followed by one of these paths, with the height to give the frame if nothing else sets it:

  • embed/map.html, height 936
  • embed/countries.html, height 2334
  • embed/within.html, height 1403
  • embed/subjects.html, height 1151
  • embed/ages.html, height 943
  • embed/time.html, height 777
  • embed/links.html, height 3464
  • embed/collections.html, height 1018
  • embed/themes.html, height 807
  • embed/descriptors.html, height 882
  • embed/jobs.html, height 1109

For example, with this site’s address in place of the dots:

<iframe src="…/embed/countries.html" title="Female share of gendered words by country"
  width="100%" height="2334" style="border:0"></iframe>

Each page also tells the page that holds it how tall it is. To have the frames fit their charts, add this once to that page, after the frames:

<script>
addEventListener("message", e => e.data && e.data.tgsEmbed && e.data.height > 0 &&
  document.querySelectorAll("iframe").forEach(f => f.contentWindow === e.source &&
    (f.style.height = e.data.height + "px")));
</script>
